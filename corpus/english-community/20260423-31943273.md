# Three Forms of AI Anxiety and How to Solve Them: Starting from Chapter 3 of the Tao Te Ching

*Author: **立正** | **Published 2026-04-21** | **[See original](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/anxiety)** | **Translated by Superlinear Bot*

---

## The Shape of the Problem

When we talk about "AI anxiety" today, we often treat it as a personal psychological problem: am I too sensitive, am I learning too slowly, has everyone else already grasped some secret.

But in practice, if you only say "I feel anxious about AI," you find there is nowhere to begin. "AI" itself is an empty concept, and the anxiety has no concrete object. A lot of AI anxiety does not come from the technology itself. It comes from the way the technology is displayed, ranked, sold, and circulated.

Chapter 3 of the Tao Te Ching opens with three sentences that correspond precisely to three forms of AI anxiety today:

> Do not exalt the worthy, and the people will not contend. Do not prize goods that are hard to obtain, and the people will not steal. Do not display what is desirable, and the people's hearts will not be disturbed.

Laozi is speaking in reverse. What he means is: if you exalt the worthy, prize rare goods, and display what is desirable, you produce contention, theft, and disorder. Translated into today's language:

- Exalting the worthy → identity anxiety
- Hard-to-obtain goods → material anxiety
- Displaying what is desirable → information anxiety

These three sentences are not saying people should have no abilities, society should have no good things, and people should have no desires. They are saying that when a society keeps holding certain things high up so that everyone circles around them comparing, chasing, and fighting for them, hearts become disturbed. What becomes disturbed is not the things themselves, but the relationship between people and the things.

## 1. Identity Anxiety Caused by Exalting the Worthy

"Do not exalt the worthy, and the people will not contend."

"Worthy" here does not simply mean capable people. It means people publicly set up as a standard. What Laozi opposes is not ability, but packaging a certain kind of ability into a social ranking so that everyone is pulled into comparison.

The "exalting the worthy" of the AI era is everywhere. "10x engineer," "AI-native talent," "people who can use AI and people who cannot are two different species," "in the future there are only two careers: people who train AI, and people who are trained by AI." Every such line is setting up a new standard of "worthiness."

What these lines produce is not knowledge but identity judgment. They stop people from asking, "Which part of the work in front of me can be transformed by AI?" and instead make them ask, "Am I an obsolete person?"

This is identity anxiety. Its defining trait is that it turns concrete problems into overall self-negation.

The original problem might only be: I still don't know how to let AI help me organize materials, write code, do data analysis, generate plans, review contracts, build workflows. But identity anxiety turns it into: I, as a person, am no longer good enough.

Once a problem turns into an identity problem, it becomes very hard to act. Action requires a concrete object, and identity anxiety has no concrete object. You don't know where to start; you only know that you are "not AI enough," "not advanced enough," "not enough like a person of the future." So many people's response is not to learn, but to imitate.

- Others show off their prompts, so I also save prompts.
- Others buy AI courses, so I also buy AI courses.
- Someone says a certain model is strong, so I immediately sign up.
- Someone says a certain role is about to disappear, so I start to doubt my own career again.

This is the result of "exalting the worthy": people build capabilities not based on the needs of their own work, but in pursuit of an externally constructed "advanced identity."

Applied to AI, "do not exalt the worthy" does not mean refusing to learn from advanced people. It means not treating labels like "AI expert," "AI native," or "super individual" as your own goal. The genuinely useful questions should be smaller:

- What is the work I repeat the most every day?
- Which kind of task drains the most of my attention?
- At which steps do I often get stuck?
- Can AI help me cut this step in half?
- How do I judge whether the AI's output is correct?

These questions point toward action. Identity anxiety points toward comparison. The thing to watch out for most in the AI era is letting technical learning turn into an identity contest.

Our community also [divides AI competency into four roles (Consumer - Explorer - Builder - Architect)](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/competency), but we point to very concrete gaps and how to close them:[#AI User 与 AI Builder 的 5 个差距，和背后具体技术原因 | 科技大厂打工人版](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/ai-mastery). These all point directly to action.

## 2. Material Anxiety Caused by Hard-to-Obtain Goods

"Do not prize goods that are hard to obtain, and the people will not steal."

"Prize" here does not simply mean high price. It means giving too much weight to what is scarce. The more something is emphasized as "hard to obtain," the more easily people lose judgment as they circle around it. The AI era has no shortage of "hard-to-obtain goods."

- Beta-testing access for a certain model.
- An invite link for a certain plugin.
- The prompts inside a certain paid community.
- The "insider playbook" some founder talks about.
- A course advertising "the last window for ordinary people."
- Some tool claiming it lets you "make money automatically with one click."

Together they create a feeling: what really matters is elsewhere, in someone else's hands, in a circle you haven't entered. That is material anxiety. These "hard-to-obtain goods" don't even need a real barrier to entry; they just need to be waved around constantly.

Take harness engineering. Even though [very few people can actually explain what it is](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/news/everybody-talks-about-it-nobody-knows-what-it-is-harness-engineering), you will still see all kinds of "AI pioneers" claiming they have applied harness engineering to their own business and produced 10x results. Claude (the crayfish) is the same. Many one-person-company bloggers urge their followers to replace human workers with Claude, and solemnly share how they orchestrate several instances of Claude to complete AI upgrades for their business, completely ignoring the fact that [Claude is actually unsuitable for ordinary people as an entry point and also unsuitable for professionals to reliably handle complex tasks](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/openclaw).

Material anxiety is not only about money. More accurately, it is "resource anxiety": am I falling behind because I'm missing a certain tool, account, course, channel, or opportunity? This kind of anxiety pushes people toward consumption rather than construction. Many think they are learning AI, but they are actually buying the feeling of "I'm not falling behind."

- Subscribe to a model, anxiety eases for a few days.
- Buy a course, anxiety eases for a few days.
- Join a community, anxiety eases for a few days.
- Save a pile of prompts, anxiety eases for a few days.

But a few days later, new models, new courses, new leaderboards, new case studies, and most importantly, new terms and concepts appear again, and the anxiety restarts. The reason: you bought a sense of contact, not capability. The real value of AI is not in how many tools you own, but in whether you can embed tools into your own problems:[#为什么所有人都在学名词，但真正拉开差距的是动词](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/verb)

With the same model, some people generate empty marketing copy, while others use it for sales-lead filtering, customer-interview summarization, code review, contract-risk annotation, research-material archiving, knowledge-base Q&A, and operational workflow automation. The difference is not in the tool, but in problem definition, context, evaluation criteria, and feedback loops. Applied to AI, "do not prize hard-to-obtain goods" means not mistaking scarce resources for core capability.

What is scarce today may become the default tomorrow. What is genuinely hard to replace is your understanding of a domain, the data you have access to, your mastery of workflows, your ability to judge whether results are good or bad, and whether you can turn a one-off success into a stable process.

So when facing AI tools, don't ask first, "What is the strongest one right now?" Ask first, "Which of my problems is worth solving with AI?" Then ask, "How strong a model does this problem need? What data? What validation? Where should humans remain in the loop?"

Most of the time, you don't need the most expensive, the newest, or the hardest-to-obtain thing. What you need is to apply an ordinary tool to a concrete scenario and iterate on it until it genuinely saves time, reduces errors, and raises output.

Material anxiety makes people hoard tools. Action makes people build processes.

## 3. Information Anxiety Caused by Displaying What Is Desirable

"Do not display what is desirable, and the people's hearts will not be disturbed."

"Display" here means "to make visible," "to place before people's eyes." "What is desirable" refers to things that, at a glance, make people want to chase and possess them. The strongest stimulus in the AI era is not any single model itself, but the continuous parade of model-related displays.

- Every day there are new demos. Every day there are new leaderboards. Every day there are new papers. Every day there are new products.
- Every day there is "just released." Every day there is "will completely change the industry." Every day there is "you must know this."

This creates an illusion: I must stay at the top of the information stream at all times, or I will fall behind. This is information anxiety. Its core is not that there is too much information, but that every piece of information hints that you should react immediately.

- See a model release, and you feel you need to study it.
- See a tool demo, and you feel you need to sign up.
- See a case study go viral, and you feel you need to replicate it.
- See news about a job being replaced by AI, and you feel you need to rethink your life.
- See someone build something amazing with AI, and you feel you've already fallen far behind.

The problem is that most information does not come with full context. An AI demo usually does not tell you: how many times it failed, how much it cost, how long the input materials took to prepare, who verified the output, whether it can be reliably reproduced, or what edge cases emerge when you put it into a real business.

A viral case study usually does not tell you either: in which scenarios it applies, in which it does not, where the underlying data comes from, which segment of the work is actually saved, and who bears the risk.

So information anxiety makes people "see a lot" but "understand little." This is "display what is desirable, and people's hearts become disturbed."

Hearts are disturbed not because people lack self-control, but because attention keeps being opened up and never closed back down. Every message opens a new thread: this matters too, that matters too, can't miss this, need to research that.

Does anyone dare say they are not learning AI? With other things, we can say we're fine without them. But AI — do you dare say you don't need to understand it?

The result is: the more you try to stay on top, the less solid ground you have. So, **the key to learning AI is not to chase the top of the information stream, but to get to the bottom of it.**Don't try to stand permanently at the crest of the wave. The crest shifts every day. You need to understand how the water flows. I can openly say I [don't use Claude](https://youtu.be/h_yCYBRzbVw), [don't use fine tuning](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai/sections/336476/lessons/1247928), [don't use LangChain](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/agentic-ai-frameworks), [don't use MCP](https://youtu.be/kwwjR6HHJPM), because I have understood the essence of these concepts, and I know they are not right for me.

Applied to AI, "bottom" refers to the foundational questions:[#Knowledge Bank](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources)

- What types of tasks is AI actually suited for?
- Why does it generate content that looks plausible but may be wrong?
- Which tasks can AI complete independently, and which must have human review?
- Why do good inputs affect outputs?
- What roles do context, data, constraints, examples, and evaluation criteria each play?
- How does an AI workflow go from "works once" to "works reliably"?
- In your industry, which tasks are text-intensive, rule-intensive, repetition-intensive, and judgment-intensive?

These questions are less exciting than "latest model release," but they build durable capability. The problem with chasing hype is that hype always makes you feel the answer lies in the next piece of information. Real learning shows you that the answer lies in the concrete problems you keep working on.

## The Shared Structure of the Three Anxieties and How to Address Them

Identity anxiety, material anxiety, and information anxiety look different, but their structure is the same.

- Identity anxiety keeps you fixated on **who other people are**.
- Material anxiety keeps you fixated on **what other people have**.
- Information anxiety keeps you fixated on **what other people know**.

All three pull attention away from your own action. You stop handling your own problems and start comparing other people's positions, resources, and information. Their common mechanism is to direct your attention toward comparison with others rather than onto the concrete work in front of you. Once comparison begins, there is no endpoint, because someone is always moving faster, has newer tools, or has more cutting-edge information.

This is the mechanism of AI anxiety: external change is fast, personal paths are unclear, comparison targets keep appearing, and feedback from action is scarce. Without feedback from action, people can only rely on information stimulus to judge whether they are safe. But information stimulus does not bring a sense of safety. It only generates demand for the next round of stimulus.

Read in reverse, Laozi's three sentences are the prescription: do not exalt the worthy, do not prize hard-to-obtain goods, do not display what is desirable. But this "do not" is not about avoiding the external world. It is about shifting the center of gravity back from "external standards" to "what I am actually doing."

Below are three concrete practices.

### Practice 1: The Opposite of Anxiety Is Action

The essence of anxiety is that the mind runs ahead of the body. You are imagining a bad situation that hasn't happened yet, the fear mechanism is triggered, but because there's no enemy to fight and nowhere to flee, the energy runs in circles inside the body and turns into anxiety.

The way out is not "don't think," but "do." As soon as you start doing something concrete, attention is pulled back to the present, and anxiety naturally recedes.

In the AI context, concrete action could be:

Pick a real problem from your actual work and try to solve it with AI. Finish reading a paper you've been meaning to read in its original form, not some blogger's second-hand summary. Take a workflow you've been using for a year, pull it apart, and redo it to see which steps can now be replaced by AI. Give a small AI talk to someone you know, which forces you to articulate what you actually understand.

None of these are grand plans. They are small, concrete actions with a clear start and end. Finish one, and the anxiety lessens by one step. Not because you removed the source of anxiety, but because you turned the abstract "AI anxiety" into the concrete "I'm working on this project."

Abstract things make people anxious; concrete things don't. If you don't know where to start looking, you can begin with [#AI Architect学员项目总结](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/share-your-projects/ai-architect-summary).

### Practice 2: Do Not Chase Hype

Hype is a commercial product. The people who manufacture hype, whether media, KOLs, or vendors, all have clear commercial interests. The more anxious you are, the more money they make. Once you see this clearly, you stop taking every "breaking news" headline at face value.

Set up a simple filter: does this have any direct bearing on what I'm currently doing?

If not, make a note and keep working on your own thing. If yes, go one layer deeper: read the original source, check the actual tests, don't stop at titles and summaries.

This filter will cut out the vast majority of information. Only what remains is worth your time.

Many people mistake "keeping up with hype" for a professional attitude. But people actually doing AI work spend most of their time chewing on concrete technical problems, not scrolling Twitter. Scrolling Twitter won't get anything built; it only tells you what others are doing.

Knowing what others are doing and actually building something yourself are two different things. The former is consumption, the latter is production. Anxiety almost always comes from consuming too much and producing too little.

It is genuinely striking: almost no chase after hype ends well, yet people keep flinging themselves at it like moths to a flame. There must be some deep psychological and sociological mechanism driving it. Only when we understand that mechanism can we get out of the loop.*

### Practice 3: Get to the Bottom, Don't Try to Stay on Top

"Stay on top" in the AI space is an impossible task. Every day there are new papers, new models, new tools, new frameworks. No human attention bandwidth can sustain that pace. If your strategy is "keep up with everything," you are guaranteed to be anxious and produce nothing.

Switch strategy: get to the bottom, understand the foundational layer.

The foundational layer changes much more slowly. The basic structure of the Transformer, the attention mechanism, the basic principles of RLHF, the agent loop, the underlying logic of context engineering: once you understand these thoroughly, most surface-level "new things" become immediately legible, and you don't have to relearn them every time.

"Chasing what's new" is horizontal expansion: cost grows linearly, returns diminish at the margin. "Going deep" is vertical construction: the upfront cost is high, but once you break through, you can digest everything built on top of it quickly.

A few concrete practices: read fewer surveys and more original papers, since surveys are pre-chewed; when you hit a concept you don't understand, push one layer deeper. For "what is an agent," don't stop at the definition; chase down "how it differs from a function call" and "in which scenarios it works and in which it doesn't." Pick two or three directions to go deep on; give up "wanting it all." You are not a researcher, you don't need to cover every branch. Apply what you learn to real projects; knowledge you don't use evaporates.

When you have a grip on the foundations, surface-level hype no longer damages you. Others are anxious about when the next model will ship; you are thinking about where the capability boundaries of the current model lie. These are two different modes of existence.
