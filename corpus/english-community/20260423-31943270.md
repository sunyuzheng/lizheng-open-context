# Why Everyone Is Learning the Nouns, but the Real Gap Comes from the Verbs

*Author: **立正** | **Published 2026-04-15** | **[See original](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/verb)** | **Translated by Superlinear Bot*

---

In the process of teaching AI, we have repeatedly run into a frustrating phenomenon.

Every time we talk about Context Architecture, task decomposition, or the judgment logic of human-AI division of labor, the audience nods along. But the questions we get most often after class are always: Can you talk more about LangChain? Can you teach RAG? Can you add a session on fine-tuning?

We have encountered a more direct version of this in enterprise training. A senior producer challenged us to our face: "Have you ever done a complex project that goes beyond a Demo?" The implication being, if you haven't even built complex projects, what gives you the right to teach us?

These two things look different on the surface, but underneath they are the same problem.

## The market is buying nouns

LangChain is a noun. RAG is a noun. Fine-tune is a noun. Harness is a noun.

Nouns have several traits that make people naturally feel "this is something worth learning":

**Nouns have boundaries.** They have GitHub repos, documentation pages, version numbers. Either you know it or you don't, and the judgment is very clean.

**Nouns can be tested.** They go on your resume, in the skills section, asked about in interviews. "Do you know LangChain?" has a clear right or wrong answer.

**Nouns carry the implication of "hard skills."** The human brain has a deep tendency: things that can be named are the "real" things. A named technology sounds more valuable than an unnamed methodology.

So when you tell the market "we teach AI methodology," the market automatically files it onto the "soft skills" shelf, next to "communication skills," "leadership," and "thinking frameworks." The stuff on that shelf feels reasonable, but not urgent, not must-learn.

Meanwhile, "LangChain," "RAG," and "fine-tune" get placed on another shelf: "technologies to learn." People are willing to pay for what's on that shelf, feeling that learning it makes them competitive.

**This is a misclassification, but it is extremely stubborn cognitively.**

## But what actually decides success or failure is verbs

Here's a reality check: in 2025, plenty of people have already learned the principles of RAG, can put together a demo, and can explain the workflow clearly in a technical interview. But ask them: in a real project with a hundred thousand lines of code, does your RAG actually work?

Most people go silent.

Because the question was never "do you know RAG." The real questions are:

- Is your context management any good? Is the information that should be loaded actually loaded? Is the noise that shouldn't be loaded filtered out?
- Is your task decomposition correct? Throw a big task straight at the AI and it will go off the rails. How do you break it down?
- Is your judgment on human-AI division of labor accurate? Should this step be done by AI independently, or does it need human intervention?
- Is the consistency of your output guaranteed? When twenty AI sessions are simultaneously modifying different parts of your project, how do you ensure they don't conflict?

These are all verbs. Decompose, judge, manage, route, diagnose, distill. None of them have a GitHub repo, none can be pip-installed, none can be written into the skills section of a resume.

But they are what determines whether your project succeeds.

**Nouns are parts. Verbs are the ability to assemble. You buy a pile of the best parts, and if you can't assemble them, it's still just a pile of parts.**

## Why people can't see the value of verbs

This isn't an IQ problem, it's a problem of cognitive structure. Specifically, there are three layers of reasons:

**First, people can only see their current bottleneck.**

Someone who hasn't yet been wrecked by AI in a complex project has a linear mental model: I don't know how to use AI right now -> because I don't understand these tools -> once I learn these tools -> I'll be able to use AI.

In this model, the bottleneck is "missing tool knowledge," a handful of nouns they don't yet recognize. You tell them "no, what you're missing is methodology," and they just can't understand it, because they haven't yet experienced "knowing all the tools but still having the project spiral out of control."

This is identical to the history of software engineering. Junior engineers think the hard part is learning React or Kubernetes, assuming that mastering the framework will let them write good software. Only after being beaten up repeatedly by architecture problems in complex systems do they realize the framework was never the bottleneck; design was. You cannot get them to believe this before the beating.

**Second, good methodology is invisible when it's working.**

When a project succeeds, everyone feels "AI is amazing" or "our team is strong." Nobody says "it's because our Context Architecture was done well."

When a project crashes, everyone attributes it to "the tools aren't good," "AI isn't mature enough," or "our requirements are too complex." Nobody says "it's because we didn't do context management well."

Tool problems are visible: errors, crashes, no output. Missing methodology is invisible: you don't know what you don't know. So **people always pay for visible problems, never for invisible ones.**

**Third, nouns create the illusion of "I've learned it."**

Finish a RAG tutorial and you feel you know it. Sit through a LangChain talk and you feel you understand it. Nouns give you a clear checkpoint: before learning you didn't know it, after learning you do, and the progress is palpable.

But methodology has no such checkpoint. You don't suddenly "learn context management" on some given day; it's a judgment that grows gradually through practice. This gradual feel makes people think "isn't this just experience? I'll naturally get it if I do it enough."

"You'll naturally get it if you do it enough" may be true. But doing it with methodology as a guide vs. doing it blindly without methodology differs in accumulation speed by a factor of ten. That's also experience, but if you haven't lived through the contrast, you can't understand the gap.

## Our real dilemma in teaching

Frankly, this problem was deeply frustrating for us for a while.

We have spent a lot of time polishing our methodology system: how to layer Context Architecture, the concrete specifications for task decomposition, the judgment framework for human-AI division of labor, how to write Skill Files so AI output becomes stable and reusable. These are things we have validated round after round in real enterprise training, real product development, and real student projects.

But we always run into feedback like: "Can you talk more about concrete tools?" "I already understand the methodology, I want to learn something hard."

You say you understand the methodology. Let me ask you: what's the structure of your Skill File? How many layers does your Context Stack have? Have you drawn your Delegation Spectrum? When AI output is bad, have you done a Failure Trace? Was it insufficient context, unclear instructions, or a task that should have been decomposed but wasn't?

Most people can't answer these questions. Not because they are weak, but because these capabilities have never been treated as "technologies to learn." They get filed under "soft improvements," grouped with "work experience," "intuition," and "sense," turning into something you feel you'll eventually have but are never in a rush to learn.

And that is precisely the most expensive illusion.

## Garry Tan's "Thin Harness, Fat Skills"

Garry Tan of Y Combinator recently wrote [an article](https://x.com/garrytan/status/2042925773300908103). Its core argument: among people using the same model, the efficiency gap can reach 100x. The gap isn't in model intelligence, it's in architecture. He summarizes this architecture into five concepts: Skill Files, Harness, Resolvers, Latent vs. Deterministic, and Diarization.

Notice what he did: he turned every component of a methodology into a noun.

Skill File isn't called "write down your experience as documentation." Resolver isn't called "load different contexts based on task type." Diarization isn't called "have the AI read all the materials and write a synthesized judgment." Each has a proper name, a clear definition, and a concrete usage.

The result? The engineering community reads it and immediately feels: "This is something I need to learn."

What he did, in essence, is **put noun clothing on verbs**. The methodology is still the same methodology, but because every component now has a name, it jumps from the "soft cognition" shelf to the "hardcore technology" shelf.

This is excellent cognitive interface design, and it's worth learning from.

## Nouns and verbs are not opposites

I'm not saying nouns don't matter. RAG is worth learning in some situations ([#我们为什么不教 RAG：对其原理与未来的深入思考 / Why We Don’t Teach RAG: A Deeper Dive into Its Principles and Future](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai/sections/336476/lessons/1247928) ). LangChain is useful in some situations ([#为什么学习Agentic AI的第一步是忘记所有框架？ ](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/agentic-ai-frameworks) ). The architectural design of a Harness is of course a key technology.

What I'm saying is: **Nouns are landmarks on a map. Verbs are how you walk between the landmarks. If you've collected all the landmarks but don't know how to walk, you can't get anywhere.**

The reverse is also true: if you only know how to walk but don't recognize any landmarks, you can't reach your destination either.

The real capability is: knowing what problem each of these nouns (tools, frameworks, concepts) solves, **and** knowing how to assemble them together in a real project (decompose, judge, route, diagnose, iterate).

Nouns give you the parts. Verbs give you the assembly capability. You need both. But the market only prices nouns, not verbs. So most people have far more nouns than verbs, a floor covered in parts, and nothing built.

## What this means for you

If you're learning AI, ask yourself one question:

**The last time you truly got stronger, was it because you learned a new tool, or because you screwed something up in a real project and then figured out why?**

Most people's honest answer is the latter.

That "figuring out why" process is exactly practicing verbs. The problem is most people treat this process as "paying tuition" rather than "learning a technology." It has no name, no course, no certificate, so it isn't treated as a technology.

But it is a technology. Just a technology that hasn't yet been adequately named.

I can't fight human nature, so what I can do is give these verbs names. Context Architecture, Context Stack, Context Resolver, Delegation Spectrum, Failure Trace, Skill Files, Agentic Loop: these aren't marketing terms. They are concrete methods we've distilled from large amounts of real teaching and project practice, methods that can be learned, practiced, and tested.

Naming them isn't to make them sound more impressive. It's because things without names don't get taken seriously.

Once it has a name, you can ask yourself: "Have I built my Context Stack?" "Have I drawn my Delegation Spectrum?" "When AI output was bad last time, did I do a Failure Trace?" You now have a set of concrete, checkable checkpoints.

That is the power of nouns. Not because the nouns themselves are magic, but because they make verbs visible, learnable, and testable.

That is also why we keep insisting on teaching methodology, while continuously creating clear names for each component inside that methodology.

**Because in the end, nouns and verbs are not two opposing choices. The best state is: learn nouns to acquire parts, practice verbs to acquire assembly capability, and then give the verbs a noun too, so they won't be forgotten.**
