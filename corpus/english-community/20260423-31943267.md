# In the AI Era, VCs Aren't Turning Into Media Companies—They're Becoming Narratives, Connectors, and New Financial Partners

*Author: **立正** | **Published 2026-04-08** | **[See original](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/aivc)** | **Translated by Superlinear Bot*

---

## A Popular but Mistaken Analogy

Benedict Evans once said something that has been widely quoted: "a16z is a media company that monetizes through VC." At the end of 2025, a16z officially announced its New Media strategy, hired an entire editorial team, acquired the podcast network Turpentine, and began producing content daily, with the goal of providing founders with "instant wins on the narrative battlefield."

A view began circulating in the industry: **In the AI era, VCs are essentially media companies.**

I think this judgment is half wrong. Not because the direction is wrong, but because the wording is imprecise, and differences in precision lead to entirely different strategic choices.

**Media and Narrative are two completely different things.** Media is an industry that requires independence, scaled distribution, continuous production, and audience management capabilities. It has its own professional barriers: editorial judgment, understanding of distribution algorithms, and control over content cadence. Among top global VCs, the number that have managed to operate media above a passing grade can be counted on two hands. a16z spent more than a decade, lived through the launch and shutdown of the Future website, before finally finding its own path. And strictly speaking, what they do is not media in the traditional sense.

Most VCs going into media is suicide. You don't have TechCrunch's distribution scale, nor the craft of a professional content team, and even less are you able to maintain the independence that media requires, because your interests are completely bound up with the objects you report on.

But if we swap the term "media company" for *"narrative company"*, everything becomes clear.

## The First Card: Narrative Company

The difference between narrative and media is this: media requires ongoing content production and distribution capabilities, while narrative requires **finding the right story, for the right company, at the right time.**

This happens to be exactly what VCs are best at.

What VCs do every day is judge trends, build frameworks, tell stories to LPs, and explain to the market why a particular sector is about to take off. This capability will be more important in the AI era than at any time in the past, for the following reason:

**The differentiation window for AI startups is shrinking rapidly.** When Cursor hits $100M ARR in 12 months, and Lovable, Bolt, and ElevenLabs all race to prove their growth rates, any given feature can be copied in a matter of days. Q1 2026 data shows that 80% of global venture capital ($242B) flowed into AI, but the graduation rate from seed to Series A has dropped from a historical 50% to under 30%. The supply side is extremely crowded.

In this environment, whether you can win attention and build a cognitive advantage in the market becomes a matter of survival. But note: what's needed here is not hiring a PR firm to run a TechCrunch piece, nor hiring a growth team for paid acquisition. Those are jobs for specialized agencies, for actual media companies, for ad agencies. **Startups should not compete with professional players on these dimensions.**

What VCs can provide is upstream: **Story/Market Fit**—helping startups find the core narrative that makes the market understand you, remember you, and choose you.

a16z itself actually spells it out clearly in its New Media manifesto: what they offer is not content production, but "launches as a service"—helping startups "win the internet for a day" at critical moments. This is not a media capability; it is a narrative capability. Helping you find the story, validate the story, and ignite the story at the right moment.

For mid-sized and small VCs, you don't need to become a full-stack content empire like a16z. What you need to do is become the person who understands narrative best in your investment domain. You know what LPs care about, you know what trends the market is chasing, you know which framework can make a complex technical story instantly intelligible. This capability is highly transferable: from helping a company pitch to investors, to helping it pitch to customers, to helping it pitch to candidates.

**Core claim: the VC's first role is not to "help founders do media," but to "help founders define narrative." Execution-layer PR, content, and advertising should be left to professional agencies; strategic-layer story definition and cadence control is where VCs have comparative advantage.**

## The Second Card: Connector-as-a-Service

AI is rewriting the shape of companies.

According to Carta's data, the median headcount at seed-stage startups has dropped from 6.4 in 2022 to under 3.5. The median for Series A companies has fallen from 57 to 47. More extreme cases: some AI-native companies have reached $100M ARR with teams of 20-50 people, while traditional SaaS companies needed 250 or more to reach the same scale. Investor Cathy Gao captured a consensus view: "Doing $60M ARR with 30 people was unimaginable before."

The leaner the team, the more obvious the capability gaps. A 100-person company used to have HR for recruiting, BD for partnerships, and an admin team to run events. Now, in a 15-person company, the CEO may also be the product manager and chief growth officer.

Agents can help you write code, do customer support, and run data analysis. But an agent cannot help you meet, at a dinner, the person who will open the door to a large customer; it cannot persuade a hesitant candidate in the talent market to come over; it cannot organize, at an industry conference, a workshop that brings potential partners together.

These things share one common feature: **they require horizontal connection capabilities, not vertical depth.** A 15-person AI startup can go extremely deep in its own vertical, but it lacks the perspective, the network, and the incentive to maintain a cross-company relationship web.

VCs have this capability naturally. A fund with 20-30 portfolio companies has, in the synergies between them, an asset in itself. Helping company A's product land company B as its first customer, helping company C recruit a key engineer from company D: these are not charity, they increase the value of the entire portfolio.

*At my previous company Statsig, I got a direct feel for the value of this kind of connector. Statsig was a Sequoia-backed company in the US, but I experienced that backing mostly through the personal relationships of our investor, Mike Vernal. Our ability to win the OpenAI deal, for example, came from a Mike Vernal introduction. Later in our Series C, when the company brought in IconiQ, I noticed their support was far more systematic: they introduced us to service firms, connected us with portfolio companies to explore partnerships, invited the CEO to industry dinners, and even directly introduced large customers.*

I call this role **Connector-as-a-Service**: not running operations for the startup (that is the startup's own responsibility), but providing the **cross-organization coordination capabilities**.

Concretely, this includes three types of services:

**Talent network.** For lean-team companies, every hiring decision is life or death. What a VC can do is not run HR for the startup, but maintain a talent pool across portfolio companies. An excellent engineer laid off by one company might be exactly what another company urgently needs. This matters even more in the AI era, because talent density is more critical now than ever before.

**Business connections.** Helping portfolio companies become each other's customers, suppliers, or partners. An AI agent company and an enterprise tools company, paired together, can yield 1+1>2. The VC is the only party with the vantage point to see these connection possibilities.

**Collective infrastructure.** Legal, finance, and compliance are basic capabilities that each become a burden for a 15-person company. A VC can centrally procure these services for the portfolio, using scale to push costs down while ensuring quality.

## The Third Card: A New Kind of Financial Partner

This is the most radical and most original of the three cards. It touches on the deepest business-model assumptions of the VC industry.

**Some background first.** The traditional VC return model relies almost entirely on capital gains: put in a check, wait for the company to IPO or be acquired, and capture the appreciation. Behind this model is an implicit assumption: good companies should grow fast, keep raising, and eventually head toward IPO or a large M&A.

But the AI era is changing the premises of this assumption.

Mobile internet addressed **concentrated demand**: social, e-commerce, ride-hailing, food delivery; each a billion-user market. But what AI is increasingly unlocking is **long-tail productivity demand**. An AI tool that helps veterinary clinics optimize scheduling, a system that manages supply chains for dry cleaners, a vision model that does quality inspection for small manufacturers: these are all profitable businesses, but their ceilings may not be high enough to support an IPO narrative.

The data also supports this judgment. AI is making software building extraordinarily easy, which means products will cover more and more long-tail niche markets, but also means it is hard for anyone in any given market to reach enough scale for a winner-take-all outcome. The graduation rate from seed to Series A is falling, but at the same time, many small companies are reaching profitability very early.

The optimal outcome for this kind of company is not an IPO, but one of two things: **being acquired**, or **quietly making money and continuously distributing dividends.**

Here's the problem.

Traditional VC fund structures do not support a dividend model, and the reasons are not just legal and tax-related (though the LP/GP structure does favor capital gains). The more fundamental reason is **information asymmetry**.

A VC invests in a company; if that company pays you a dividend every year, how does the VC verify that the dividend base is real? Founders can inflate costs, run related-party transactions, and shift profits. Fraud under a dividend model is easier to pull off than under an equity-appreciation model, and harder for audits to catch. This is the fundamental reason dividend models have been unworkable in the VC domain for so long.

**But what if the VC is also the "connector company" described above?**

If many of the startup's external services—legal, finance, HR, business partnerships—are all obtained through the VC's network, then the VC actually has very high visibility into the company's cost structure. The suppliers you introduced, the customers you connected, you know their Stripe data and their real revenue. You are no longer a remote observing investor, but a partner embedded in the capillaries of the company's operations.

In that case, the core obstacle for the dividend model—information asymmetry—is substantially dissolved.

What does this mean? It means the AI era may give rise to **an entirely new investment-vehicle structure**: not the traditional 10-year closed-end fund, not reliant on IPO exits, but something closer to an evergreen fund or a revenue-share fund. The VC gets an informational edge through deep operational involvement, then realizes ongoing returns through a dividend mechanism.

This is not science fiction. The global market size for Revenue-Based Financing has grown from under $5B in 2023 to nearly $10B in 2025, with annual growth exceeding 60%, making it one of the fastest-growing alternative models in startup financing. Meanwhile, in traditional PE, creating value through deep operational involvement and using mechanisms like dividend recapitalization to recover capital before exit has long been standard practice. McKinsey research shows that PE funds focused on operational value creation have IRRs on average 2-3 percentage points higher than their peers. What VCs need to do is combine these two trends: use the continuous-recovery logic of RBF together with the operational depth of PE, to construct a new structure suited to the long-tail startup ecosystem of the AI era.

What VCs need to do is not copy the PE model, but fuse the VC's sector judgment and narrative capability with the PE's operational depth and financial discipline, to create a new species suited to the long-tail startup ecosystem of the AI era.

## A Trinity: The VC That Isn't Called VC

To summarize the argument.

VCs in the AI era face a moment of identity reinvention. When capital is no longer scarce (Q1 2026 saw $300B in global VC investment, the highest single-quarter figure on record), VCs must answer one question: **Besides money, what else can you give founders?**

My answer is three roles:

**Narrative company**—helping founders define their story and find story/market fit, not replacing agencies in content and distribution. **Connector company**—providing ultra-lean AI startup teams with the horizontal coordination capabilities they cannot build themselves: talent networks, business connections, collective infrastructure. **Financial partner**—using operational depth to gain an informational edge, making dividend models viable in VC, and fitting the many long-tail companies of the AI era that "don't need an IPO but make money steadily."

There is a progressive logic among these three roles: narrative capability wins you the best deal flow, connector capability takes you deep into company operations, and operational depth opens the door to entirely new financial models. Together they form a flywheel.

This thing probably shouldn't be called Venture Capital anymore. Capital is no longer the core competency. What to call it? Maybe **Venture Partner**, maybe **Builder's Guild**, maybe **Nimble PE**, maybe there isn't an accurate name yet. But the direction is clear: from capital provider to a trinity service provider of narrative, connection, and finance.

In an era when a founder can build $100M in revenue with 15 people, investors proving their value can no longer rely on a single check.
