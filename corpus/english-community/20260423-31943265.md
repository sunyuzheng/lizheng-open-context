# The AI Native Organization: When Execution Costs Drop, the Org Chart Must Be Rewritten

*Author: **立正** | **Published 2026-03-26** | **[See original](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/ai-native-orgs)** | **Translated by Superlinear Bot*

---

## TLDR

- AI is making individuals 15-40% faster (super individuals see 10x+). Yet companies show zero measurable gains (NBER, Goldman Sachs).
- The bottleneck is not the tools, but the organization.
- Two root causes: **incentive misalignment** (paying for time, not output) and **organizational friction** (meetings, approvals, and alignment processes were not designed for AI speed).
- **In our corporate training practice, we observed three samples of organizational rebuilding: one threw all development into a subsidiary, one ran 3-5 person Pods achieving 10x speed, and one laid off everyone who writes code and kept only AI Architects.**
- An AI Native organization requires: end-to-end product ownership, teams organized by trait rather than job title, and Context infrastructure as the moat.
- Flattening alone will fail (see Spotify, Zappos, Amazon). You need new incentives, new evaluations, and new systems.
- The real competition is not Claude Code vs. Cursor, but who first builds an AI Native organization.

## 1. Numbers that are unexpected yet make sense.

In February 2026, the NBER (National Bureau of Economic Research) published a study covering four countries: the US, UK, Germany, and Australia. After surveying 6,000 CEOs, CFOs, and executives, the conclusion was: **70% of companies claim to be using AI, but nearly 90% of them say AI has had no measurable impact on their productivity or employment.**

In March, Goldman Sachs analyst Ronnie Walker wrote in an earnings analysis: at the macroeconomic level, no meaningful correlation was found between AI adoption and productivity. 70% of S&P 500 management teams discussed AI on their earnings calls, but only 10% provided quantitative data on AI's impact, and only 1% quantified AI's contribution to earnings.

At the same time, efficiency gains at the individual level are real. McKinsey's research consistently shows that knowledge workers using AI-assisted tools typically see task-level productivity gains of 15%-40%. Goldman's own report also noted that in the two specific scenarios of software development and customer service, median productivity improvements were around 30%.

I believe most members of the community, if they are using AI in an agentic AI manner, can feel multi-fold efficiency gains. People like Grape Brother (Yage) or Andrej Karpathy have even experienced hundred-fold boosts.

**Individuals got faster, but companies did not.** This is one of the most important paradoxes of 2026.

## 2. Productivity has improved, so why haven't companies gotten faster?

Most people attribute this to "AI tools aren't good enough yet" or "employees haven't learned to use them." But the real bottleneck is not the tools, it is the organization.

Marx had a judgment that has been repeated countless times but remains precise: **The forces of production determine the relations of production, but the relations of production in turn constrain the forces of production.** When a new productivity tool appears, if the relations of production (meaning organizational structure, incentive mechanisms, and collaboration patterns) do not keep up, the power of the tool gets consumed by the organization itself.

AI is running into exactly this situation. Specifically, there are two structural bottlenecks.

### Bottleneck One: Full-time employees' incentive structure is not aligned with productivity gains

This is an observation from @陈然: full-time employees are paid for time, not for actual output.

What does this mean? If you are an engineer and AI lets you finish in three days what used to take two weeks, what do you gain? Your salary stays the same. You'll most likely just be given more work. And if you use the saved time to slack off, you might actually have an easier time.

Under this incentive structure, full-time employees have no motivation to push AI to its limits. Their incentives are not aligned with maximal productivity gains. A Faros AI study of 10,000 developers has confirmed this: **after adopting AI, developers wrote more code and finished tasks faster, but team delivery speed and business results showed no measurable improvement.** EY's survey also points out that 88% of employees use AI at work, but only 5% are actually using AI to transform how they work. The vast majority just use it for basic tasks like search and summarization.

**Munger said he has always remembered that incentives are the most important factor, yet every year he discovers he has underestimated their influence. In the context of AI improving productivity, incentive misalignment must be the most decisive bottleneck.**

### Bottleneck Two: Organizational friction consumes individual efficiency gains

Traditional large-company organizations were designed for information collaboration and coordination. Meetings, reporting, cross-team alignment, approval workflows: these are not bugs, they are features. They exist because in an era when execution was expensive, you had to make sure every execution was correct, which required heavy upfront alignment.

But AI has pushed execution cost to a tipping point. If you can build a prototype in a few hours, the logic of spending three days in meetings to align requirements collapses. You might as well just build it first and let user feedback speak.

The problem is, once you speed up your own work, the other steps in the process don't speed up. You finish the code, and code review sits in a queue for two days. You ship a product and wait a week for legal approval. One person's efficiency gain gets flattened by the bottleneck nodes in the whole pipeline. What's worse, when AI lets you eliminate certain "necessary" work (such as no longer needing so many cross-team alignment meetings), those whose value used to depend on such meetings feel uneasy. The old relations of production fight back.

**One interesting finding: METR's 2025 study found that experienced developers using AI tools actually took 19% more time to complete tasks, even though they themselves believed they were 20% faster.** Why? Because they spent too much time integrating and validating AI-generated code against existing systems. This is not an AI problem; it's a problem of workflows that have not been redesigned for AI.

**AI is a sports car, but you are driving it through alleyways. The problem is not the car; it's the road.**

## 3. Those who are rebuilding the road

In our corporate training work, as well as in the industry, we have observed several cases trying to "rebuild the road." They represent three different approaches to organizational restructuring.

### Case One: Large companies spinning off the execution layer — "Move all development to a subsidiary"

A friend of mine is a GM at a top domestic tech company, and he is doing something radical: moving all development positions to a subsidiary, and technically keeping only a core GenTech delivery team in the department. This team's responsibility is not to write code, but to serve as a bridge between technology and business: gathering requirements, managing the AI toolchain, maintaining core architecture, and handling operations and coordination.

The logic behind this is: if AI pushes the marginal cost of producing code toward zero, then "writing code" is no longer a core competency. Core competency shifts to judgment on technical direction, the ability to orchestrate AI tools, and an understanding of business context. By spinning off the execution layer, a department can become leaner, faster, and more focused.

### Case Two: Large companies incubating small teams internally — "Pod model"

A big-company team we're currently partnering with on training has chosen a gentler but equally ambitious path: outside the existing organizational structure, spin up new 3-5 person small teams, similar to the Pod model Meta is running. These teams do not follow traditional reporting lines or old development processes. Instead, they are driven by a clear product goal and iterate quickly.

The goal of these small teams is not to achieve 100% of what a large team does, but with 10% of the headcount to achieve 80% of the effect, at 5-10x the speed. We are providing custom training for them, and we believe that after this training, 5-10x speed is the floor.

[Grape Brother's (Yage) deep news article today systematically analyzes what Meta is doing in Reality Labs](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/news/meta-ai-builder-pods). In a developer tools team of about 1,000 people, they eliminated traditional functional titles like engineer, designer, and PM, and unified them into three roles: AI Builder (executor), AI Pod Lead (small-team lead), and AI Org Lead (organizational manager). The Pod Lead handles day-to-day execution, while the Org Lead handles performance and promotion, and the latter explicitly introduces AI assistance for performance evaluation.

### Case Three: A Silicon Valley startup — "Lay off everyone who writes code"

Another Silicon Valley startup we are training goes even further: they plan to lay off everyone whose primary job is writing code, and only keep the role they call **Conductor**. In our course, [we call the similar role ](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/share-your-insights/competency)[**AI Architect**](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/share-your-insights/competency).

The core work of these Conductors/Architects is not to write code, but to:

- **Orchestration**: orchestrating and managing AI Agents so they collaborate efficiently
- **Boundary Setting**: setting success criteria and finish lines for AI Agents
- **Measurement**: building effective measurement systems to evaluate the quality of AI output
- **Context Management**: organizing and maintaining the context information AI needs

Their view is blunt: **If an engineer is still writing code by hand, it means this person hasn't learned how to really use AI well.** (Of course, this statement has its preconditions; it applies more to new products, new code, and new companies.)

Writing code is to software engineering as laying bricks is to architectural design: you need architects, not bricklayers. This trend is already resonating across the industry. Addy Osmani (one of the engineering leads of Google Chrome) has summarized this evolution path: **from Coder, to Conductor, to Orchestrator** — from someone who writes code, to someone who directs AI to write code, to someone who orchestrates multiple AI Agents working in parallel.

## 4. Understanding from first principles: what should an AI Native organization actually look like?

After looking at these cases, let's step back and think about this from first principles.

AI has massively boosted individual productivity, but has hit two organizational-level bottlenecks: incentive misalignment and organizational friction. So what is the key to breaking through?

### Principle One: End-to-End ownership of the product

The core problem of traditional organizations is: **too many people are accountable for process, and too few are accountable for outcomes.**

Frontend engineers are responsible for frontend code quality, backend engineers for backend, PMs for PRDs, designers for mockups. Everyone does their part well, but the final product might still be a mess — because no one is accountable end-to-end for the product's user experience and business outcomes.

The first principle of an AI Native organization is: **the core team must take end-to-end ownership of the product.** Not accountability to the code, not accountability to the mockups, but accountability for whether users can get value from the product and whether the product can succeed in the market.

### Principle Two: Build teams by Trait, not by Job Family

Traditional team composition is organized by Job Family: you are a frontend engineer, he is a backend engineer, she is a designer. But in the AI Native world, AI is blurring these functional boundaries. A good engineer can produce decent design with the help of AI, and a technically inclined PM can build a prototype directly with AI.

A more reasonable way to organize teams is by **Trait** rather than Job Title. A few key Traits include:

**Builder / Pirate** — the person who turns ideas into reality. Their core capability is execution and speed. One company calls this role the Pirate, meaning: do whatever it takes to hit the goal as fast as possible, and leave the rest to the Architect.

**Architect** — the person who makes the system scalable and maintainable. What the Builder makes might run, but the Architect ensures it keeps running, and runs at scale. They focus on system design, technology choices, and long-term technical debt management.

**Taste Maker** — the person with aesthetic sense and taste. In an era when AI generates massive amounts of content, "what is good" becomes an increasingly critical judgment. This person controls quality, improves the experience, and ensures that the product is not merely usable, but delightful to use and something users want to use.

**Signal Reader** — the person who understands user needs and can capture signals from the market. They continuously do user research (quantitative or qualitative), continuously answering one question: is what we're building what the market actually needs?

**Decision Maker** — the person who can make decisions under uncertainty and continuously generate effective initiatives. In a small team, there are no layers of approval to help you reduce decision risk; you need someone who can form judgments with incomplete information and bear the consequences.

An ideal AI Native small team is 3-5 people, where every person carries some combination of the Traits above, but each has a clear dominant Trait. They do not define their scope of work by Job Title; they flex based on what the team most needs at the moment.

### Principle Three: Context is the competitive edge

In AI Native organizations, one thing is severely underrated: **Context**.

The effectiveness of AI tools depends heavily on the quality of the input context. The same model, given a vague instruction versus given a complete set of clear specifications, historical data, and user feedback, produces output of vastly different quality. This means: **your value in an organization depends more and more on how high-quality the context you can provide to AI (and colleagues) is, not on how many lines of code you personally produce.**

Through our corporate training work, Grape Brother (Yage) and I have gradually developed a methodology around **Context Architecture**, covering three layers:

- **Context Org Chart**: defining what context a task or project needs, and who is responsible for producing and maintaining that context. Where our context is scattered, how it should be organized, and how it should be progressively exposed to AI.
- **Context Architecture**: designing how the whole team's context flows — from raw information, to a structured knowledge base, to a format AI can consume. Grape Brother (Yage) has partially open-sourced this on GitHub: [https://github.com/grapeot/context-infrastructure](https://github.com/grapeot/context-infrastructure)
- **Context Toolchain**: selecting and configuring the toolchain that supports context flow — documentation systems, knowledge bases, MCP integrations, AGENTS.md, and so on

The detailed expansion of this methodology will be shared in future articles. But the core idea is simple: **the moat of an AI Native organization is not who uses better AI tools, but whose context infrastructure is stronger.**

## 5. Why most "flattening" attempts fail

By this point, you might be thinking: isn't this just making teams smaller and reducing management layers? Haven't many companies already tried this?

Yes, they have. But history tells us most "flattening" attempts fell short of their intended effects, because they confused two things: **compressing management layers** and **eliminating the need for management**.

Spotify's Squad model is a classic case. A Squad is also a small cross-functional team with a high degree of autonomy. But Spotify later admitted that autonomy without alignment tends to slide into fragmentation — individual Squads make locally optimal decisions, but the cost of cross-Squad coordination keeps climbing, eventually offsetting the speed advantage of small teams.

Zappos' Holacracy experiment went further. It tried to replace management layers entirely with self-organization, only to find that managerial work (assigning responsibilities, resolving conflicts, making trade-offs) did not disappear. It merely shifted from managers with formal authority to people without formal authority, becoming more hidden and less efficient.

Amazon's approach is to widen managers' spans of control and cut middle layers. This does reduce cost, but when a manager oversees 15-20 people, the quality of people development and career growth drops noticeably.

**The key to an AI Native organization is not the surface change of "making teams smaller," but rebuilding a whole set of supporting systems** — including incentive mechanisms, evaluation methods, context infrastructure, and cross-team coordination mechanisms. Meta's choice to roll out the Pod model in a 1,000-person department rather than company-wide itself reflects this insight: **changing organizational form is easy, but making the new form actually work requires substantial supporting investment.**

## 6. If you are a decision maker, what you should do now

This is not a "wait until AI matures" problem. It is an organization design problem that needs to start today.

**Step one: find a small enough product line and set up an AI Native small team as an experiment.** 3-5 people with end-to-end responsibility for a product or a module of one. Give them clear outcome metrics (not process metrics), and give them sufficient tools and authority. Do not make them go through traditional processes.

**Step two: invest in Context infrastructure.** Not by buying more AI tools, but by structuring the team's knowledge, processes, and decision history so AI can consume this context. The impact of good context infrastructure is often more significant than switching to a stronger model.

**Step three: redesign the incentive system.** If your employees are paid by time and evaluated by process, they have no incentive to radically change how they work with AI. Consider pay-for-outcome models, or at minimum, ensure AI-driven efficiency gains translate into benefits employees can feel, whether that's more autonomous time, larger bonuses, or faster promotions.

**Step four: cultivate Architect / Conductor talent.** The scarcest people in the future are not those who can write code, but those who can orchestrate AI, manage context, and make judgments under uncertainty. This capability must be deliberately cultivated — in our AI Competency Model, it corresponds to L5-L6 level capabilities, far beyond what "knowing how to use ChatGPT" covers.

## 7. Conclusion: the backlash from old relations of production

Every major productivity revolution in history has encountered backlash from the old relations of production.

When the steam engine appeared, craftsmen smashed the machines. After assembly lines spread, unions struck in resistance. After computers entered the office, it took middle management twenty years to be truly redefined.

AI is no exception. When you try to restructure an organization with AI, you will encounter all kinds of resistance: people whose core work is "attending meetings" will oppose reducing meetings; people whose value lies in "coordination" will oppose shrinking teams; people whose identity is tied to "writing code" will resist role transformation.

But productivity wins in the end. The only questions are when and how.

The companies that complete their organizational transformation first will open up a 5-10x per-person efficiency gap. And in the face of that gap, tool-level optimizations — Copilot vs. Cursor, Claude vs. GPT — are hardly worth mentioning.

**The real competition is not about who uses the better AI tools, but about who first builds an AI Native organization.**
