# Five Gaps Between AI Users and AI Builders, and the Technical Reasons Behind Them | A Big Tech Worker's Take

*Author: **立正** | **Published 2026-04-10** | **[See original](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/ai-mastery)** | **Translated by Superlinear Bot*

---

Over the past two years I've trained thousands of people from top tech companies. The conclusion is getting clearer:

When most people say "AI is unreliable," they are not describing the model's capability. They are exposing the level of their own methodology.

Same model. Some people feel it can only produce "roughly okay" output; others have already turned it into a work system that delivers stably, iterates continuously, and can even take on entire tasks. The difference is not in the tool, it is in the method. AI amplifies the gap between people, not narrows it.

I call the top AI users AI Builders. Between an AI Builder and an ordinary user there are at least five quantifiable capability gaps. These gaps are not about talent, not about years of experience, and not merely about "writing better prompts." They are five deeper methodology gaps.

## **TLDR**

1. **Whether AI output is directly usable** depends on what you let the AI see (Document-First + Context Curation), not on how fancy your prompt is
2. **Whether you can steadily improve AI output quality** depends on whether you can diagnose why the AI is wrong: hallucination, context saturation, ambiguous instructions, or capability boundaries each require completely different fixes
3. **Whether you can hand a full task to AI** depends on whether you have built the closed loop (Agentic Loop) that lets AI execute, check, fix, and re-check on its own
4. **Whether usage experience compounds into team-level leverage** depends on whether you have a Context Architecture and continuously accumulated [AGENTS.md](http://agents.md/) / [MEMORY.md](http://memory.md/)
5. Once the four above are in place, **AI evolves from an execution tool into a thinking partner**. This is an emergent capability, the natural outcome once context density crosses a threshold

## Two Worlds

We often hear comments like this: AI-generated content can't be used directly. Ask it to write code, it always has errors. I've tried it, it's unreliable.

But another world has already emerged.

Boris Cherny, the lead of Claude Code, said on X in January 2026: "For over two months straight, 100% of my code has been generated by Claude. I don't even make small tweaks anymore. Yesterday I shipped 22 PRs, the day before 27, all done by Claude."

This is not an isolated case. Since the second half of 2025, nearly all of Karpathy's code has been AI-generated. I have personally seen @Yan Wang 鸭哥 burn through more than 10 billion tokens in a single quarter, produce millions of lines of code, deliver real business value, with nearly all the core output done by AI.

This is not the difference between two tools. It is the same tool producing completely different results under two different ways of working.

Many people summarize the gap as: how many tokens you burn, how many agents you can control, how long you can keep the AI running. These matter, but they are not the underlying explanation. What really determines the outcome are the five points below.

---

## Gap 1: Whether AI output is directly usable

An ordinary user gives AI a task, iterates five or six rounds, still fails to get what they want, and ends up finishing it by hand. An AI Builder's output can go straight into the workflow most of the time; occasional tweaks are needed, but accuracy keeps improving with use.

The most common misjudgment here: people think the gap comes from the prompt.

It isn't.

Here's an analogy. You ask a very smart new hire to draft a proposal. You only said "help me draft a proposal," then you shake your head at what they produced: no, that's not what I meant. You add a sentence, they revise, still wrong. After five rounds you can't take it anymore and do it yourself.

Where's the problem? Not that this person isn't smart. They simply don't know what you want: your aesthetic preferences, your judgment principles, the team's default rules, past tradeoffs, acceptance criteria, all sitting in your head, none of it given to them.

AI is no different. What determines output quality is not how elegantly that one prompt is written, but what information the AI actually sees while executing the task.

**Document-First。** Project specs, style guides, acceptance criteria, historical decisions, constraints. All of these should be written as structured documents AI can read. For AI, information it cannot read is information that does not exist. Your standards only live in the human brain, so AI will always be guessing. It is not "unintelligent," it just cannot access the inputs it needs to judge.

**Context Curation。** More context is not always better. What matters is signal-to-noise ratio. This is the same logic as a search engine: too much garbage dilutes the key information, and the result drifts. Many people assume they wrote a bad prompt, when in fact the problem is that the context is dirty. What should be there isn't, and what shouldn't be there got stuffed in.

**Outcome-Oriented Instructions.** Don't micromanage AI step by step as if it's an intern. You wouldn't tell a smart person "first open Excel, then write a title in cell A1"; you would say "I need a comparison table of three options with cost, time, and risk dimensions, and a final recommendation." Clearly define the outcome: what the output looks like, what criteria it must meet, what boundaries it must not cross. Not teaching it "how to do it," but specifying "what counts as correct."

This is the first step that turns AI from a chat partner into a production system.

## Gap 2: Whether you can systematically judge quality and diagnose problems

Ordinary users look at AI output and usually settle for "seems roughly okay." When something goes wrong they cannot pin down where the problem is and can only swap prompts and try again. That's like someone who can't fix a car and simply tries restarting it when something breaks.

AI Builders are different. They have clear acceptance criteria and the ability to diagnose failures. Instead of "try a few more times," they locate the root cause and fix it precisely.

This is what determines whether a person's AI skills can keep improving. Whether you improve depends on whether you can debug.

**Evaluation Design。** Every task should have clear, executable acceptance criteria. Not "looks okay," but: does it meet condition X? Does it avoid error class Y? Does it cover the key edge cases? Does it match the expected format, style, and constraints? Once the criteria are clear, AI is no longer just "generating," it can self-check before output. This quality gate is simple but extremely effective.

**AI Debug。** This is a point I want to stress. AI failure is not a single problem, it is several fundamentally different classes of problems. You cannot apply one universal fix to every AI output issue, the same way you can't treat every disease with the same medicine.

- Missing context → hallucination (add information)
- Context window saturation → information loss (compress, split)
- Ambiguous instructions → directional drift (rewrite the goal)
- Model capability boundary → the task itself exceeds what the current model can do reliably (switch model, switch tool, reduce task complexity)

Without root cause diagnosis you're running on luck; stable debugging requires genuine understanding of how AI works. This echoes what I said in my earlier ChatGPT piece: how deeply you understand a tool's underlying mechanics determines how far you can push it.

**Observability Design.** Black-box output is nearly impossible to debug. You have to make the intermediate process visible: tool-call trajectories, staged artifacts, self-check results, failure reasons, corrective actions. Don't just look at the final answer; look at how it grew. Once the process is observable, problems surface early instead of only at final-output failure.

## Gap 3: Whether you can delegate a full task to AI

Ordinary users work in "single-step mode": give one instruction, look at the result, add another line, look again. While the AI works, the human is actually tied to it, attention continuously occupied. You think you're using AI for leverage, but you're actually babysitting it in person.

AI Builders do something different: they hand over a goal all at once instead of remote-controlling the process step by step.

Boris Cherny again. In his Fortune interview he said something I think is very accurate: "I've never felt this much joy at work. Because all the tedious work is done by Claude, I can do creative things, and think about what I want to build next."

That is real time leverage.

What he hands to AI is not the next action but a whole task: goal, boundaries, criteria, available resources. Then he lets AI decompose, execute, check, and fix on its own. The key is not how fancy the term "multi-agent" sounds; I have seen plenty of people talk about agents eloquently while still remote-controlling AI one sentence at a time in practice. The key is three more fundamental capabilities.

**Agentic Loop。** An effective AI system is not "you do, I watch, I edit." It is: AI executes → AI self-checks → AI diagnoses → AI corrects → AI re-checks. Humans don't need to watch every step; they only need to step in once the loop has converged. This loop doesn't appear on its own; it must be designed. You have to tell the AI how to check its own output, what criteria define a pass, and how to correct on failure.

**Skill Writing。** Complex tasks can't be explained from scratch every time. You encode the approach for a class of tasks into a reusable skill document: how this type of task is usually broken down, what to check first, common pitfalls, what counts as done. You are no longer "teaching the AI this one time," you are "installing a capability module." Same logic as my ChatGPT training point: a good teacher doesn't teach from scratch every time, they build a reusable curriculum.

**Scaffolding Management.** More constraints are not always better, and neither is more freedom. When the task is unfamiliar, you need dense constraints; once the task pattern stabilizes, you should gradually remove unnecessary limits and let AI operate freely. Over-control wastes capability, over-delegation wastes time. Knowing how to build scaffolding and how to progressively remove it is a key dividing line in system maturity.

## Gap 4: Whether AI usage experience accumulates into team assets

The pitfall an ordinary user stepped into last month will be stepped into again this month. Everyone's experience is locked in their own chat history, and newcomers start from zero. It's like a company where everyone searches for information their own way, with no knowledge base, no wiki, everyone reinventing the wheel.

AI Builders do this differently: a pitfall someone hit becomes a rule, a practice someone validated becomes a template, a blind spot someone discovered joins the team's shared checklist. The system gets stronger over time instead of re-exploring every time.

Three things make this work.

**Context Architecture。** A team must design its own way of organizing context: what information goes where, what is retained long-term, what is loaded on demand, who updates it, and through which path the AI reads it. This is fundamentally a knowledge management problem, except today the audience for knowledge management is not only humans, it also includes AI.

This connects to [what I wrote in my ChatGPT article](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/chatgpt) about "Personalized & Private Search." Two years ago I said you should make your own data contextual and indexed so it's easy to search and feed to ChatGPT. Today this has evolved into [a more systematic architecture problem](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/context-infra).

[**AGENTS.md**](http://agents.md/)** / **[**MEMORY.md**](http://memory.md/)**.** The value of these files is not "two extra documents." It is that they turn experience from personal tricks into system memory. [AGENTS.md](http://agents.md/) defines the AI's behavioral norms; [MEMORY.md](http://memory.md/) captures the judgment principles, preferences, boundaries, and no-go zones that the team has already validated repeatedly. Once experience is written down, the AI's capability no longer fully depends on a single expert guiding it live; it becomes team-level infrastructure.

Remember what I said two years ago about "a good ChatGPT teacher"? I said that person needs three capabilities: strong domain understanding, understanding of pedagogy, and the ability to find real needs. [AGENTS.md](http://agents.md/) and [MEMORY.md](http://memory.md/) are how you freeze that "good teacher's" curriculum into a durable form. The teacher can change, but the curriculum stays.

**Progressive Disclosure。** Don't cram every piece of material into the context window at once. Load the most critical upfront constraints first, then introduce details gradually as the task progresses. This is both a performance optimization and, more importantly, a quality optimization. AI only makes stable judgments in a high signal-to-noise context.

## Gap 5: Is AI an execution tool or a thinking partner

Ordinary users use AI to speed up their existing workflow: writing faster, editing faster, analyzing faster. AI's role is still that of a more efficient doer.

When AI Builders reach the next stage, they see something completely different: AI is not only doing things for you, it starts offering quality judgments in domains where you aren't strong; it is not only executing your commands, it points out contradictions between your decisions and your own principles; it is not only answering questions you already had, it raises directions you hadn't thought of.

This is not a capability a single prompt can trigger. It is the natural emergence once the first four are in place.

In my ChatGPT article I introduced a concept: ChatGPT drives the marginal cost of "intelligence distribution" toward zero. That judgment was right, but what I didn't think through at the time was the prerequisite for AI to truly become an extension of your intellect.

The prerequisite is context density.

Once AI has accumulated enough density of context, including your value judgments, historical decisions, working principles, preference boundaries, and recurring tradeoff logic, it finally has the conditions to make judgments on your behalf in your blind spots. The point is not more information, but layered distillation: continuously distilling stable principles out of real work records, removing noise, keeping signal.

Once the system reaches this stage, the question is no longer "is AI reliable." The real, higher-level questions become: how deeply does this AI understand me? Where are its judgment boundaries? What context am I still missing that keeps it from judging on my behalf?

## Summary

These five gaps are not parallel, they are progressive.

The first two are the foundation: whether you can produce usable output, whether you can judge quality. The third is leverage: whether you can hand off a full task so one person can drive multiple AI instances in parallel. The fourth is the flywheel: whether you can turn experience from "personal tricks" into "a team system" so capability compounds. The fifth is the outcome: AI evolves from an execution tool into a genuine thinking partner.

Each one has corresponding technical practices, and each gap is not a matter of talent but of method.

So the judgment "AI is unreliable" is usually inaccurate. A more accurate statement: your context is not complete enough, your evaluation mechanism is not strict enough, your debug ability is not mature enough, your system memory has not been built.

Fix these, and the same AI will perform as if it were a different species.
