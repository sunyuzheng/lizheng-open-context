# Two Major Pitfalls in Learning AI: Looking for Textbooks (Only Reading, Not Doing), Copying Homework (Relying on Tutorials)

## TLDR

Most people fail to learn AI because their **learning approach was wrong from the start**:

- **AI is not a knowledge subject, but a craft.**
Understanding concepts and being able to explain them doesn't mean you can consistently deliver results.
- **Classroom-style learning creates fatal illusions.**
Mistaking "I understand" for "I can do it," procrastinating on practice, treating errors as failures rather than feedback.
- **Tutorials and toy projects don't truly build capabilities.**
They complete the most important parts for you: modeling, breaking down, validating, diagnosing, and iterating. You're just going through the motions.
- **True AI capability comes from hands-on practice and repeated trial and error.**
Turning vague requirements into tasks, defining what "usable" means, pinpointing failure causes, iterating within constraints.
- **The test is "transferability."**
If you can still deliver when switching tasks, tools, or domains, then you've truly learned.

Learning AI should revolve around **delivery capability + real feedback**. Focus on improving capabilities, not just "understanding" or "getting it to work."

## Main Article

In teaching AI, I repeatedly see two common and harmful learning pitfalls:

1. Treating AI as "knowledge" to learn, using school-style methods (understand lectures, memorize concepts, solve problems correctly)
2. Treating AI as "tutorials" to learn, relying on tutorials to do toy projects, or copying others' homework online (e.g., "How to build a personal assistant with OpenClaw")

Both approaches create a false sense of progress—"I understand""I made it work"—but true capabilities don't grow, and may even decline.

The reason is simple: **AI is not a knowledge subject; AI is more like a craft.** We must respect how crafts are learned.

## I. AI is More Like Skiing/Swimming: It Must Be Learned Through "Feel"

Learning to ski and learning to swim have one thing in common: you can hear the principles a hundred times, but if you don't get in the water, don't fall down, you won't learn.

AI is the same, because:

### 1) You're Learning "Delivery Capability," Not "Explanatory Capability"

School education is good at training: understanding, reasoning, recounting, proving, solving problems.
But what AI truly needs to train is: breaking down, trial and error, validation, iteration, trade-offs, getting results implemented.

In AI contexts, "I understand" usually only means you can explain concepts clearly, not that you can consistently deliver results.

### 2) AI's Key Capabilities Exist in "Micro-moves," Not Grand Principles

Skiing isn't learned from the phrase "keep your center stable"—it's learned through subtle adjustments of ankles, knees, and hips.
AI isn't learned from vague concepts like "provide context" or "write clear requirements"—it's learned through what you do at each step:

- How to turn vague requirements into executable tasks
- How to define acceptance criteria (what counts as "correct," what counts as "usable")
- How to get the model to reveal uncertainty rather than pretend to know
- How to diagnose failure causes and adjust strategies

These capabilities must all be trained repeatedly through real feedback,**You can't learn by "watching." Really, you can't.**

### 3) The True Sign of Learning Is "Transferability"

Someone who can swim can swim in any pool.
Someone who has learned AI can quickly get up to speed and deliver when switching tasks, tools, or domains.

If you only know one example, that's not capability, that's memory.

## II. The Biggest Problem With School-Style Learning for AI: Mistaking "Understanding" for "Mastery"

School-style learning is not entirely without value for AI—it can train thinking.
But it has a particularly dangerous side effect:

> It makes you mistake "understanding" for "mastery."

In the AI era, this illusion is fatal because what matters with AI isn't whether you can explain, but whether you can build things.

### Three Typical Harms From School-Style Learning

1. **Turning learning goals into "conceptual correctness" rather than "reliable results"**
This creates many people who "talk well but can't deliver."
2. **Over-pursuing complete understanding, delaying practice and iteration**
Skill learning most fears "I'll start once I fully understand."**The more you wait, the less likely you are to ever start.**
3. **Treating errors as deductions rather than feedback**
In school, errors are failures; in crafts, errors are data.
Without switching mindsets, you'll increasingly fear making mistakes and dare not try, losing the most important source of learning—real feedback.

In summary, in the process of learning AI, we must realize that**we cannot use exam-thinking, cannot rely on our decade-plus of classroom learning habits—rather, we must overcome these habits**. Think more about**how we learned to swim, to program, to drive, to assemble furniture, any useful skill. That's the method we should use for learning AI**.

## III. Why Tutorials / Toy Projects / Copying Homework Also Won't Teach You AI

The second type of pitfall is more common: many people learn AI through tutorials, do a bunch of toy projects, and think they "know it."

This type of pitfall is also one that ordinary courses easily lead people into, even when the instructor is well-intentioned—I demonstrate the "craft" myself, show it to you, help lower the barrier to entry. Isn't this the best teaching?

The problem is: **tutorials often outsource the most critical and valuable ****capability training****.**

### 1) Tutorials Train You in "Execution," Not "Modeling"

The truly valuable capabilities in AI aren't "following instructions"—they're:

- Topic selection: what's worth doing
- Modeling: clearly defining the problem
- Breaking down: splitting tasks into actionable steps
- Acceptance: defining verifiable standards
- Diagnosis: attributing failures to controllable variables
- Iteration: approaching usable results with minimum cost

But the structure of tutorials is usually:

> The problem is given, the path is given, the breakdown is given, the pitfalls are avoided for you, and you know what the result should look like.
What you complete is "going through the motions," not "building capability."

This is the essence of "copying homework": you're practicing copying speed, not problem-solving ability.

### 2) The "False Sense of Achievement" From Toy Projects Comes From Three Illusions

1. **Complexity illusion**: toy projects lack real-world dirty data, constraint conflicts, exception handling, deployment costs
2. **Environment illusion**: tutorial environments are carefully set up favorable scenarios by authors; real work has incomplete information, changing requirements, unreliable tools
3. **Path illusion**: tutorials imply a "standard answer"; the real world only has trade-offs and compromises

The confidence you gain in toy projects often collapses instantly in real-world scenarios.

### 3) Tutorials Also Foster Bad Learning Habits

1. **Can't start without a tutorial**: because you haven't practiced "going from chaos to structure"
2. **Treating prompts as spells**: memorizing templates that fail when tasks change
3. **Neglecting acceptance and reliability**: the goal is "getting the demo to work," not "stable delivery"
4. **Developing performance-based learning**: work you can post on social media doesn't equal productivity
5. **Distorted error attribution**: when things fail, blame the model or tools, rather than checking task breakdown, acceptance criteria, constraints, and data

The end result is: you look hardworking, seemingly capable of many things. But capabilities don't grow, anxiety only increases.

Tutorials themselves are useful. Many carefully crafted tutorials teach you a lot while you learn how to do things. But we cannot overly rely on tutorials and neglect learning itself.

To use tutorials well, we must first learn modeling, build confidence in hands-on practice and finding answers in the real world, then use well-designed tutorials to accelerate learning. But**don't mistake tutorials for learning itself. Learning is for building capability.**

### Subtopic: "Freeloading" Is an Even More Harmful Habit in the AI Era

Speaking of the harms of tutorials, let me say more about the topic of "freeloading."

We all know "freeloading" is impolite, but in the past many people were still willing to do it. Because the cost, while impolite, was "getting information asymmetry in less time"—a good deal.

In the AI era, the cognitive/habitual harm of freeloading is much greater. Most problems can already be learned by asking AI. And the questioning and judgment skills cultivated in the process are one of the few key capabilities for humans in the AI era.

Answers obtained by freeloading are actually becoming less valuable. So in the AI era, being a freeloader, besides being even more impolite (just ask AI for more questions), the truly scary thing is exchanging your valuable assets (thinking habits, judgment) for something of little value (information).

This is just like watching tutorials:**what tutorials teach you is actually becoming less valuable. But what tutorials help you skip—that's actually the most valuable part**.
