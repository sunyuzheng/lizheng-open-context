# Your Team's AI Isn't Working: The Problem Isn't the Model, It's the Context

*Author: **立正** | **Published 2026-04-09** | **[See original](https://www.superlinear.academy/c/ai-resources/context-infra)** | **Translated by Superlinear Bot*

---

## A Phenomenon You Already Know

Your team is already using AI. Cursor, ChatGPT, Copilot — the tools are all in place. But when you ask how well it works, the answer is usually "sometimes it works, sometimes it doesn't" or "fine for simple tasks, unreliable for complex ones."

Where's the problem? Not the model. Give the same model a vague instruction versus giving it** a complete set of clear project background, historical data, design specifications, and records of past pitfalls**, and the output quality will be worlds apart.

**What determines AI effectiveness isn't which tool you use, but the quality of the context you feed it.**

People are starting to realize this. There's a term in the industry called context engineering. It's not about competing on how fancy your prompts are, but on how well your context is organized. But what does "organizing context" actually mean? Most teams get stuck here.

## The Current State of Your Context

Your team's context is scattered across at least these places: discussions in Slack/Feishu, proposals in Google Docs/Tencent Docs, retrospectives in Confluence/Notion, decision records in emails, meeting notes, and experience locked inside people's heads.

These things have no structured relationships between them, no unified index, no version control. Every time someone needs AI to do something even slightly complex, they either start from scratch explaining the background to the AI (your most expensive people doing grunt work), or manually piece together context from memory (missing key information and getting bad output).

The bigger problem is that experience is evaporating. A project finishes and the experience stays in the participants' heads. When someone leaves or their memory fades, the next similar project starts from scratch. Retrospectives get written but no one reads them. Proposals get written with different formats and stored in different places each time. AI can't draw on any of this experience, because it simply isn't in a place AI can reach.

## What We Deliver: Context Architecture (Three Layers)

We help your team build a complete context infrastructure. It has three layers, each solving a different problem.

### Layer One: Context Org Chart (Conceptual Layer)

This is a diagram for managers. It shows where your team's context is currently scattered, how the pieces relate to each other, which should be called on by AI day-to-day, which should accumulate as long-term knowledge, and which need to be periodically distilled and updated.

An analogy: when a marketing agency meets a client for the first time, the most powerful thing isn't talking methodology. It's pulling out a client-specific marketing calendar. New product in September, so warm-up starts in August. Launch event in March, so February is when you contact KOLs, prepare assets, line up distribution channels, and so on.

The Context Org Chart is the marketing calendar of our trade. Your reaction after seeing it should be: "Yes, this org chart precisely describes the current state. Things really are scattered all over the place, and the way you've organized it is indeed what we should be doing."

But most teams, once they have this org chart, find they can't actually put it into practice. That's where the second layer comes in.

### Layer Two: Context Architecture (Technical Architecture Layer)

This layer solves the problem of actually managing context well. The things teams can't do on their own all live here:

**Layered management.** Not all context is equally important. We divide context into three layers of memory: working memory (what the current task directly needs), project memory (experience and decisions accumulated in this project), and organizational memory (long-term knowledge across projects). What should stay in raw form, what should be distilled into essence, and what should be periodically cleaned up — all of it follows explicit rules.

**Progressive loading.** AI's context window is limited. You can't stuff everything in at once, because it'll blow up. And you can't give it nothing, or the AI is just an empty shell. The core of the technical architecture is this: when the AI executes any task, it automatically finds and loads exactly the context it needs, no more and no less. This requires building indexes, doing classification, and designing loading strategies.

**Continuous distillation.** Raw meeting notes, Slack discussions, and design docs are raw context. An AI agent can periodically distill this raw context: what counts as effective experience, what pitfalls have been hit, which judgment criteria behind decisions are worth preserving. By refining raw context into high-quality insights, the whole team's AI capability keeps getting stronger, and the AI also becomes more efficient and precise when retrieving context.

**Standards injection.** Your team's most important standards, such as design specifications, balancing criteria, code quality requirements, and review checklists, can be written as rules the AI understands, and injected into the workflow. When employees use AI to do their work, the AI automatically uses these rules to double-check the output. The effect is equivalent to having your most senior person show up in every piece of concrete work, without actually taking up their time.

One more point: a lot of the time the difficulty in management isn't the absence of clear standards. It's that enforcing those standards takes enormous energy and resources. But once the context architecture is built, using standards to manage AI is far easier.

## A Game Design Example: How to Put Config-Table Balancing into Practice

Take card-set balancing. Traditionally, whether a deck's config table is balanced depends on a senior designer's intuition. After a junior designer finishes, they either play-test it repeatedly themselves (small sample, time-consuming), or queue up for a senior designer's review (waiting on people), or wait until launch and discover issues through live data (late, expensive, and damaging to reputation).

In our enterprise training, we break down the balance judgments in a senior designer's head into two layers of automatic checks:

- Layer one is static rules: 11 executable rules covering mana-cost curves, keyword distribution, and field completeness, running in milliseconds. Run it every time you tweak the config table.
- Layer two is dynamic simulation: an AI plays the role of a strategic player, running tens of thousands of games across three Boss environments, tallying usage rate and contribution value for each card. It runs in minutes, and is specifically designed to catch mechanical blind spots that static checks miss.

The result: designers no longer queue up waiting for senior colleagues. After changing the config table, they run it themselves and get a diagnosis in minutes. And the judgment criteria behind that diagnosis are preserved in the rule library. Every time a new blind spot is discovered, it gets added in, and the whole team automatically benefits.

**This structure isn't unique to games.** In any industry, anything that's "important but requires a senior person's judgment, and happens frequently" can be handled the same way: turn the implicit judgments in senior people's heads into explicit assets the AI can consistently enforce.

## Why Is This Hard for Your Team to Do?

Someone on your team may already be making scattered attempts: organizing a few prompt templates, building a knowledge base, writing a few automation scripts. But scattered attempts and systematic infrastructure are two different things.

The gaps sit in three places. First, not knowing how to layer and manage context (three-tier memory, distillation strategies, isolation boundaries), which is an architecture design problem. Second, not knowing how to make AI progressively load context (index design, loading strategies, context window management), which is a technical implementation problem. Third, not knowing how to make standards persistently take effect (rule injection, automatic checks, observability), which is an engineering problem.

Designing these three things requires someone with both AI engineering experience and organizational management experience. This is exactly the core capability we've gradually accumulated and honed over the past year, rolling it out across multiple enterprises.
